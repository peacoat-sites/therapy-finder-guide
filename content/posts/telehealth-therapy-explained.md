Imagine you've finally decided to talk to someone. You've been putting it off for months, maybe longer. You look up therapists in your area, and the first three you call aren't accepting new patients. The fourth is 45 minutes away and only has Tuesday morning slots. You work Tuesday mornings. So you close the browser and tell yourself you'll try again later. For a lot of people, "later" never comes. Telehealth therapy exists, in large part, to close that gap between intention and action.

## What Telehealth Therapy Actually Is

Telehealth therapy is licensed, professional mental health treatment delivered through video calls, phone calls, or secure messaging platforms instead of an in-person office. That's the simple version. The fuller picture is a bit more layered.

The therapist on the other end is a credentialed professional: a licensed clinical social worker (LCSW), a licensed professional counselor (LPC), a licensed marriage and family therapist (LMFT), a psychologist (PhD or PsyD), or a psychiatrist (MD or DO). They have the same training, the same ethical obligations, and often the same supervision requirements as someone you'd see in a brick-and-mortar office. What changes is the medium, not the credentials.

Video sessions are the most common format and most closely mirror face-to-face therapy. Phone sessions are available for people without reliable internet or those who simply prefer them. Some platforms offer asynchronous text-based therapy, where you exchange messages with a therapist over the course of days. That last format is the most debated among clinicians because the real-time connection is reduced, but it can work well for certain people and certain goals.

I've seen clients who were initially skeptical of video therapy become its biggest advocates after a few sessions. The skepticism is usually about whether anything "real" can happen through a screen. It can. The therapeutic relationship, which research consistently identifies as one of the strongest predictors of therapy outcomes, forms just as meaningfully over video. A 2018 meta-analysis published in the *Journal of Affective Disorders* found that telehealth cognitive behavioral therapy produced outcomes comparable to in-person CBT for depression and anxiety. That's not a small finding.

Telehealth isn't a universal upgrade over in-person therapy, but it's a genuine match for a broad range of people and situations.

People with physical disabilities or chronic illness who struggle with transportation are obvious beneficiaries. So are people in rural or underserved areas, where a qualified therapist might be an hour's drive away or simply nonexistent locally. Parents of young children who can't easily carve out time for travel. People with social anxiety for whom the idea of sitting in a waiting room triggers dread before the session even starts. Shift workers with unpredictable schedules. People who travel for work. All of these groups often find telehealth meaningfully more accessible.

There's also a population I want to name specifically: people who are in therapy for the first time and feel some shame or stigma about it. For some, the anonymity of being at home, not walking into a recognizable office building, lowers the barrier enough to actually show up. That matters more than it might sound.

Where telehealth is less ideal: people experiencing active psychosis, severe eating disorders requiring medical monitoring, or active suicidal crises typically need in-person or higher-level care. If you're in immediate danger, please contact the [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/) by calling or texting 988. They're available around the clock, and that service is specifically designed for moments when telehealth alone isn't enough.

Telehealth also works across many therapy modalities. Cognitive behavioral therapy translates beautifully to video, and if you want to understand the model before your first session, our detailed guide on [cognitive behavioral therapy explained](/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-explained/) walks through the core concepts. DBT skills groups have moved online with strong results. Even [EMDR therapy](/emdr-therapy-explained/), which many people assumed required in-person delivery, has been adapted effectively for telehealth with the right tools and a trained provider.

## How to Get Started: A Practical Step-by-Step Guide

Starting telehealth therapy is more straightforward than most people expect. Here's how to move from thinking about it to actually doing it.

**Step 1: Identify what you're looking to address.**
You don't need a diagnosis or a perfectly articulated problem. But having a rough sense, whether it's anxiety, relationship conflict, grief, or just a persistent feeling that something is off, helps you find a provider with relevant experience.

**Step 2: Check your insurance.**
Telehealth coverage expanded significantly after 2020, and many plans now cover it at the same rate as in-person visits. Our guide on [whether insurance covers therapy](/does-insurance-cover-therapy/) breaks down what to look for in your policy. Call the member services number on your insurance card and ask specifically: "Does my plan cover telehealth therapy with a licensed mental health provider?"

**Step 3: Search for providers.**
SAMHSA's [treatment locator at findtreatment.gov](https://findtreatment.gov/) is a free, government-maintained tool that helps you find licensed providers by location and specialty, including those offering telehealth. Psychology Today's therapist finder also has a telehealth filter. Our article on [how to find the right therapist](/how-to-find-the-right-therapist/) offers additional guidance on what to look for beyond credentials.

**Step 4: Book a consultation.**
Most therapists offer a free 15 to 20-minute phone or video consultation. Use it. Ask about their experience with your specific concern, their theoretical approach, and their availability. Fit matters as much as credentials.

**Step 5: Set up your space.**
Find a private spot where you won't be interrupted. Use headphones if you live with other people. A stable internet connection matters: aim for at least 10 Mbps upload speed. Have a backup plan (your phone's data connection) in case your Wi-Fi drops mid-session.

**Step 6: Show up for the first session.**
It will probably feel a little awkward. That's normal. Give it at least three sessions before you evaluate whether it's working.

## Telehealth vs. In-Person Therapy: An Honest Comparison

Neither format is universally better. Here's a side-by-side look at the real trade-offs:

| Factor | Telehealth | In-Person |
|---|---|---|
| Accessibility | High, no travel required | Limited by geography and transportation |
| Privacy | Variable, depends on your home | Typically higher in a dedicated office |
| Nonverbal cues | Partial, body language partially visible | Full, including posture and movement |
| Crisis response | Limited, provider can contact emergency services but can't be physically present | More direct options available |
| Scheduling flexibility | Usually better | Often more rigid |
| Technology barriers | Real concern for some | Not an issue |
| Insurance coverage | Increasingly comparable | Broadly covered |
| Modality availability | Most modalities work; some limitations with somatic therapies | Full range available |

The single biggest practical difference I've observed: people with difficult home environments, whether that means a controlling partner, noisy shared housing, or a lack of private space, often genuinely struggle to do meaningful telehealth therapy. That's not a failure of the format; it's a logistics problem worth solving before you start, not after.

## What to Expect in a Telehealth Session

The structure of a telehealth session is nearly identical to in-person therapy. Sessions typically run 45 to 55 minutes. The first one or two sessions are usually an intake, where your therapist gathers background information, discusses your goals, and explains their approach.

After that, sessions vary depending on the therapist's style and your needs. A CBT-oriented therapist might work through thought records or behavioral activation exercises with you on screen. A therapist using [Acceptance and Commitment Therapy](/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy-act/) might guide you through a values clarification exercise. Someone trained in DBT will often assign and review skills practice between sessions. The homework doesn't go away just because you're at home.

One thing some clients find helpful is supplementing sessions with structured self-reflection tools. A CBT journal or mindfulness workbook used between sessions can reinforce what you're working on in therapy. Something like the *Feeling Good* workbook by David Burns or a dedicated thought record journal can make the work between sessions more concrete. Confidentiality rules apply exactly the same way online. Your therapist is legally and ethically required to protect your information, and the platforms used for telehealth therapy, the legitimate ones, are required to be HIPAA-compliant. If a platform isn't HIPAA-compliant, that's a red flag worth asking about before you share anything personal.

## Understanding Costs and Insurance for Telehealth

Cost is one of the most common reasons people delay or skip therapy entirely. Telehealth doesn't automatically mean cheaper, but it does open up some options.

Many private insurance plans now cover telehealth mental health services at parity with in-person sessions, especially following legislation passed during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Medicaid coverage for telehealth varies significantly by state. Medicare covers telehealth therapy for beneficiaries. If you want a deeper breakdown of what different insurance types typically cover and what questions to ask your insurer, our guide on [finding a therapist that takes insurance](/finding-a-therapist-that-takes-insurance/) is a practical starting point.

If you're uninsured or underinsured, community mental health centers often offer telehealth on a sliding-scale fee basis. Some university training clinics have moved to telehealth and offer lower-cost sessions supervised by experienced clinicians. Open Path Collective is a nonprofit network of therapists offering reduced fees specifically for people without adequate coverage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is telehealth therapy as effective as in-person therapy?

For most common mental health concerns, including depression, anxiety, PTSD, and OCD, research supports that telehealth therapy produces outcomes comparable to in-person treatment. The evidence base is strongest for CBT delivered via video. For severe or complex conditions requiring close clinical monitoring, in-person or higher levels of care are often more appropriate.

### Can I get a psychiatric medication prescription through telehealth?

Yes, in many cases. Telehealth psychiatry has expanded significantly, and many licensed psychiatrists and psychiatric nurse practitioners can prescribe medication via telehealth. Federal rules around controlled substances and telehealth prescribing have shifted over time, so it's worth confirming current regulations in your state with the provider directly.

### What technology do I actually need?

A smartphone, tablet, or computer with a working camera and microphone is sufficient for most telehealth therapy platforms. A stable internet connection helps. Many therapists also offer phone-only sessions if technology access is a barrier.

### Is telehealth therapy private and secure?

Licensed therapists are required by law to use HIPAA-compliant platforms. Before starting, ask your therapist which platform they use and confirm it meets privacy standards. On your end, using headphones and finding a private space significantly reduces the risk of others overhearing your session.

### What if I have a crisis during a telehealth session?

A trained therapist will have a safety plan in place before this happens. During your intake, your therapist should ask for your address and an emergency contact so they can act if needed. If you're experiencing a mental health emergency outside of a session, contact the [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/) by calling or texting 988, or go to your nearest emergency room.
