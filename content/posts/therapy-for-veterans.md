Most articles about veteran mental health spend two-thirds of their word count explaining that war is hard. You already know that. What you probably don't know is which specific therapy types actually work for combat-related PTSD, how to access care without drowning in VA bureaucracy, and what to do if you've tried one approach and it didn't help. Let's skip the obvious part.

## The VA Is a Starting Point, Not the Only Option

The Department of Veterans Affairs is the largest provider of mental health care for veterans in the country, and it has genuinely improved its services over the past decade. The VA's mental health programs now include same-day crisis services at most medical centers, telehealth appointments, and specialized PTSD clinics. If you're eligible for VA care, use it. The price is right and the clinicians are often specifically trained in military culture.

That said, wait times vary wildly by location. Some veterans get an appointment within two weeks; others wait two months. If you're in the latter group and you're struggling, don't just wait. The VA's Community Care Program lets eligible veterans see outside providers at VA expense when wait times are excessive or the VA can't meet a specific need. Call your nearest VA medical center and ask explicitly about Community Care eligibility. Many veterans don't know it exists.

If you've left the service recently and haven't connected with VA care yet, [NAMI's veteran resources page](https://www.nami.org/) is a reasonable first stop for understanding your options before you make a single phone call.

Not all therapy is equal for combat trauma, and "just talking about it" with a general counselor often isn't enough. Here are the approaches with the strongest research base for veterans specifically:

**Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT)** is currently the gold standard for combat PTSD. It's a 12-session structured protocol that targets "stuck points," the distorted beliefs that form around traumatic events ("I should have done more," "nowhere is safe"). It requires written assignments between sessions, which some people hate and others find genuinely useful. The VA trains a large number of its therapists in CPT specifically.

**Prolonged Exposure (PE)** is the other tier-one option. It's harder to sit through because it involves revisiting traumatic memories in a controlled, graduated way, but the outcomes are strong. Studies published in JAMA and NEJM consistently show both CPT and PE outperforming general supportive therapy for PTSD. If a therapist suggests one of these and you flinch, that's normal. Do it anyway if you can.

**EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing)** has decent evidence for trauma, though it's more contested among researchers than CPT or PE. Some veterans swear by it, particularly those who find it hard to talk directly about their experiences. It's not magic; it's a structured protocol using bilateral stimulation while processing trauma. Worth trying if the first two haven't clicked.

**Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)** is less commonly discussed in veteran contexts but increasingly supported for moral injury specifically. Moral injury, the damage that comes from witnessing or participating in things that violate your values, is distinct from PTSD and often undertreated. If your pain is less about fear and more about guilt or a shattered sense of identity, ACT might fit better than exposure-based approaches.

One honest caveat: therapy type matters less than therapist quality and your own commitment to showing up. A skilled therapist doing supportive counseling will often outperform a mediocre one running a manualized protocol.

## Finding a Therapist Outside the VA

If you're going outside the VA system, [Psychology Today's therapist directory](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/therapists) lets you filter by specialty, insurance accepted, and treatment approach. Filter for "PTSD," "military," or "trauma" and look for clinicians who list CPT or PE as specific modalities, not just "trauma-informed care," which has become a catch-all phrase that means almost nothing on its own.

A few other options worth knowing:

The **Give an Hour** network connects veterans with volunteer licensed mental health professionals for free care. Quality varies, but for someone without insurance or VA eligibility, it's a real option.

**Headstrong** (formerly known as The Headstrong Project) provides free mental health treatment specifically for post-9/11 veterans and their families in several cities, with telehealth expanding availability. If you qualify by service era, it's one of the better-resourced nonprofit options available right now.

**BetterHelp and Talkspace** work for some veterans, particularly those managing mild-to-moderate anxiety or depression rather than active PTSD. I'd be honest with you though: for complex combat trauma, the asynchronous messaging format that these platforms use as a core feature is not a substitute for real-time structured therapy. Use them as a bridge, not a solution.

## The Peer Support Factor

Something the clinical literature tends to underweight: peer support works. Veterans consistently report that talking with someone who has been in service carries a different weight than talking with a civilian clinician, regardless of how competent that clinician is. This isn't a knock on civilian therapists. It's just a real phenomenon.

The VA's Peer Specialist program employs veterans with lived mental health experience as part of care teams. Ask your VA care coordinator if your facility has them. Outside the VA, organizations like the Veterans Crisis Line (dial 988, then press 1) staff veteran-specific responders, and the Pat Tillman Foundation, Team Red White & Blue, and Mission 22 all build community structures that reduce isolation, which is itself a major risk factor.

If you're a reader who helps veterans, rather than being one: stop steering people only toward clinical resources. Sometimes the guy from their unit who got help and is doing better is more persuasive than anything in a brochure.

## Practical Tools You Can Use Between Sessions

Therapy once a week leaves 167 hours where you're on your own. A few tools that aren't gimmicks:

The VA's own **PTSD Coach app** is free, clinician-developed, and actually good. It includes symptom tracking, grounding exercises, and psychoeducation that reinforces what happens in session.

For structured self-reflection work, the [The Mindfulness and Acceptance Workbook for Anxiety](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1572244993?tag=contentportfo-20) by Forsyth and Eifert is a solid ACT-based resource that several therapists I've worked alongside recommend as between-session support. Sleep is treated like a soft issue and it isn't. Trauma-related nightmares are one of the most debilitating PTSD symptoms and one of the most undertreated. If you're in therapy and nobody has addressed your sleep yet, bring it up directly. Image Rehearsal Therapy (IRT) is a specific protocol for trauma nightmares with good evidence. Not every therapist knows it, but it exists and it works.
