Most therapy sessions are 50 minutes long. That's not an arbitrary number, and it's not going to change because you feel like you need more time. It became the default for practical reasons, and for most people working through most things, it works. But "most" isn't "all," and understanding why the standard exists, and when to break from it, will help you make a smarter decision before you ever book your first appointment.

The 50-minute "therapy hour" dates back to Freudian psychoanalysis. Analysts scheduled patients back-to-back, using the remaining 10 minutes to write notes and reset. That structure stuck. Insurance companies then standardized reimbursement around it, clinicians trained around it, and the field calcified. Today it's both a billing convenience and a genuinely reasonable amount of time for most talk therapy approaches.

What nobody tells you upfront: the right session length depends less on how much pain you're carrying and more on what kind of therapy you're doing, how your nervous system handles sustained emotional work, and whether your insurance cares.

## The Standard Formats and What They're Actually For

Here's how session lengths typically break down across different therapy types and contexts:

| Session Type | Typical Duration | Common Use Case | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard therapy ("50-minute hour") | 45-55 min | Individual CBT, talk therapy, supportive therapy | Most insurance plans reimburse this length |
| Extended individual session | 75-90 min | EMDR, somatic therapy, intensive work | May require prior auth; higher copay |
| Psychiatric med check | 15-30 min | Medication management only | Not therapy; billed differently |
| Couples/family therapy | 60-90 min | Relational work, family systems | Often runs longer by design |
| Group therapy | 60-90 min | Peer support, skills groups (DBT, etc.) | Lower cost per person |
| Intensive outpatient (IOP) | 3+ hours/day | Crisis stabilization, addiction recovery | Multiple sessions bundled daily |

One thing worth knowing: when a therapist bills for a "90806" or "90837" CPT code, they're telling your insurance the session was 53 minutes or more. A "90834" is 38-52 minutes. This matters because your insurance's explanation of benefits will show which code was used, and that determines your reimbursement. If your therapist is billing 90837 but only seeing you for 40 minutes, that's a discrepancy worth raising.

## Why 50 Minutes Is Usually Enough (But Not Always)

Fifty minutes is enough time to surface a problem, work through a piece of it, and land somewhere that isn't just raw and open-ended. That's not nothing. For approaches like [Cognitive Behavioral Therapy](/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-explained/), the structure of a 50-minute session actually fits the model: check-in, agenda-setting, skill work, homework assignment. Tidy.

But some therapy types don't fit that mold.

EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing) is the clearest example. Stopping an EMDR session mid-reprocessing isn't just inefficient, it can leave a client in a destabilized state. Trained EMDR therapists almost universally recommend 90-minute blocks, especially during the active reprocessing phases. The EMDR International Association addresses this in its practice standards, and most experienced practitioners won't attempt full reprocessing in under 75 minutes.

Somatic therapies, prolonged exposure for trauma, and certain psychodynamic approaches have similar reasons to run long. Emotional activation takes time to build, and cutting a session short right when something important surfaces doesn't serve the work.

Couples therapy is its own situation. Two people, twice the history, twice the reactivity. Sixty minutes is the floor; 90 is better if you can swing it logistically. Some couples therapists who practice the Gottman Method specifically structure their sessions at 90 minutes because the model's interventions require more time to implement than 50 minutes allows.

## Who Should Consider Non-Standard Lengths

Shorter sessions (30-45 minutes) can make sense in a few specific situations: children under 10, people with significant attention difficulties, individuals in a maintenance phase of therapy who are largely stable and just checking in periodically, or someone in a medical setting where brief behavioral interventions are the goal. This isn't a lesser version of therapy. It's calibrated to what actually helps.

Longer sessions aren't just for trauma work. People who have long commutes and can only realistically get to a therapist once a month sometimes negotiate 90-minute sessions to make the visit more substantive. That's a practical workaround, not a clinical requirement. Whether it works depends entirely on the therapist's approach and the client's capacity for sustained focus.

**Example:** Assume someone is doing weekly CBT for generalized anxiety. Standard 50-minute sessions cost them a $30 copay each. They feel like progress is slow and wonder whether 90-minute biweekly sessions would be more effective. Under their plan, a 90-minute session is billed as an extended session with a $60 copay. At 26 sessions per year (weekly), they'd pay $780 annually at the standard rate. At 13 sessions per year (biweekly, 90 minutes), they'd pay $780 as well. Same cost, half the contact. For CBT specifically, weekly contact is often more effective because skills consolidation happens between sessions. The biweekly 90-minute option isn't obviously better here, and the reduced frequency may actually slow progress. Session length and session frequency are both variables, and they can't be swapped freely.

## The Cost Dimension

Insurance coverage for therapy session length is not uniform. Most commercial plans cover the standard 50-ish minute session (CPT 90837) without issue. Extended sessions may require documentation of medical necessity. If you're paying out of pocket, therapists typically charge per session regardless of whether it's 50 or 90 minutes, though extended sessions cost more. Rates vary widely by region, specialty, and provider experience, so you'll want to confirm directly.

If cost is a barrier, community mental health centers and federally qualified health centers often offer therapy on a sliding-scale fee. SAMHSA's treatment locator at [findtreatment.gov](https://findtreatment.gov/) lets you search for these providers by ZIP code and filter by payment type, which is a faster path than calling around blindly.

One common misconception: longer sessions aren't more efficient just because there's more time. Some people hit a point of diminishing return around 60-70 minutes where they're emotionally exhausted and no longer processing productively. Knowing your own limits here is real self-knowledge, not weakness.

## Talking to Your Therapist About Session Length

Most people never ask about this. They show up for whatever the therapist's default is and assume that's the right amount. That passivity makes sense, it's new territory, and therapists are the experts. But therapists also set defaults for scheduling convenience, not necessarily because they've assessed your specific needs.

If you want to have a productive conversation with a therapist about session length, the [First Therapy Session checklist](/first-therapy-session-checklist/) on this site gives you a structure for the whole intake conversation, including what to ask about format and fit. Worth reviewing before your appointment.

A few direct questions you can actually ask:

- What length are you booking me for, and why?
- Does the type of therapy we'll be doing work better with more time per session?
- If I find 50 minutes consistently feels incomplete, what are my options?
- How does my insurance handle extended sessions under your billing?

Therapists respect these questions. They signal that you're engaged rather than passive. The therapeutic relationship works better when you're a participant in the structure, not just a recipient.
