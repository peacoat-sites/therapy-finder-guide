Most people who come to me with depression and anxiety don't even realize they have both. They describe feeling exhausted and unmotivated, then mention in the same breath that they can't stop worrying, that they lie awake running disaster scenarios, that dread follows them from room to room. They think it's all one thing. Often, it is, in the sense that the two conditions are so frequently tangled together that treating one without acknowledging the other tends to backfire.

Here's something I tell people right away: having depression and anxiety simultaneously is genuinely common. A 2015 review published in *Dialogues in Clinical Neuroscience* found that roughly 60% of people with depression also meet criteria for an anxiety disorder. That number should probably be more reassuring than it is, because when you're in it, you still feel like you're uniquely broken.

You're not. But you do need a slightly different approach than someone dealing with just one or the other.

## Why Treating Both Together Changes Everything

The instinct a lot of people have, including some well-meaning clinicians, is to address whichever condition looks more severe and assume the other will follow. Sometimes that works. More often, it doesn't, and here's why: depression and anxiety feed each other through different but overlapping mechanisms. Depression tends to pull you inward, toward withdrawal and inactivity. Anxiety tends to push you outward, toward hypervigilance and avoidance. When they run concurrently, the result is often a person who is simultaneously paralyzed and wired, which is one of the more exhausting states a human being can be in.

A therapist who's only targeting the depression might use behavioral activation, encouraging you to do more, get out, build momentum. Good strategy, in theory. But if your anxiety is driving avoidance, simply telling yourself to "just do it" will feel impossible, and when it doesn't work, the depression gets worse. You blame yourself for failing at something that was never going to succeed without addressing both pieces.

The flip side is equally true. Working purely on anxiety reduction through relaxation and exposure work won't touch the anhedonia, the flat emotional landscape, the inability to feel motivated by things you used to love. You'll be calmer, maybe, but still hollow.

What actually works is a therapist who explicitly holds both in mind and builds a treatment plan that addresses the interaction between them.

There's a lot of noise about therapy types online. Let me be honest about what has the strongest research base for this specific combination.

**Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)** is the most well-studied treatment for both depression and anxiety, including when they co-occur. It works by identifying the thought patterns that sustain both conditions, catastrophizing, black-and-white thinking, negative self-appraisal, and then systematically testing whether those thoughts hold up. The 2013 edition of David Burns's *Feeling Good* is still one of the best-written lay explanations of CBT principles, and his follow-up *When Panic Attacks* covers the anxiety side specifically. I often recommend both to people who want to understand what they're walking into before their first appointment.

**Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)** has gained serious traction over the past decade, and honestly, for people whose depression and anxiety are both rooted in relentless self-criticism and psychological rigidity, I think ACT sometimes outperforms traditional CBT. It doesn't ask you to challenge your thoughts so much as to change your relationship with them. You notice the thought, you don't let it drive. A good introductory self-help workbook for this is *The Happiness Trap* by Russ Harris, which is available on Amazon (note: this site may earn a small commission on purchases made through links here) and gives you a solid foundation before or alongside working with a therapist.

**Behavioral Activation**, used within CBT or as its own modality, is specifically designed for the withdrawal and inertia of depression, and recent meta-analyses suggest it also reduces anxiety symptoms, particularly social anxiety. It's less about "doing more" in a vague sense and more about strategically reintroducing meaningful activity in graded steps.

**Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT)** is worth mentioning specifically for people who have experienced multiple depressive episodes. It was developed at Oxford in the 1990s for recurrence prevention, and there's good evidence it reduces anxiety symptoms alongside depressive relapse. Jon Kabat-Zinn's *Full Catastrophe Living*, also on Amazon, is the foundational text, though it's a commitment to read.

The research here is genuinely solid across these approaches, but I want to be clear: no therapy works for everyone, and the specific modality matters less than the relationship you build with a competent therapist. A mediocre CBT practitioner will get worse results than a skilled ACT therapist, and vice versa.

## What to Actually Look For in a Therapist

This is where most advice fails people. Everyone says "find someone you connect with," which is true but nearly useless when you don't know what you're walking into.

When you're dealing with depression and anxiety together, you want a therapist who explicitly says they work with comorbid conditions. That word, comorbid, just means two or more conditions present at the same time. Ask directly in your initial consultation: "Do you have experience treating depression and anxiety together, and how do you approach that when one is making the other worse?" A therapist who gives you a clear, specific answer is worth pursuing. One who gives you a vague, reassuring non-answer is a yellow flag.

You also want someone trained in at least one of the evidence-based modalities mentioned above, not someone who describes their approach as "eclectic" without being able to tell you what that means in practice. Eclectic can be fine, but it can also be a polite way of saying they'll improvise.

Practical places to start your search: [Psychology Today's therapist directory](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/therapists) lets you filter by specialty, insurance, and location, and most listings include a personal statement from the therapist that tells you a lot about their style quickly. If cost or access is a barrier, [SAMHSA's treatment locator](https://findtreatment.gov/) can help you find sliding-scale and community-based options.

On cost: therapy for depression and anxiety typically runs $100 to $250 per session out of pocket depending on your city and the therapist's credentials. With insurance, your copay might be $20 to $60. Telehealth has genuinely expanded access, and platforms like Alma or Headway specialize in connecting people with insurance-paneled therapists. I'm not affiliated with any of these, but I've seen them work well for people who assumed they couldn't afford care.

## What the First Few Months Actually Look Like

You might be wondering what you're signing up for, practically speaking. Most evidence-based treatments for comorbid depression and anxiety involve somewhere between 12 and 20 structured sessions, though some people do shorter or longer work depending on severity and history.

The first two or three sessions are mostly assessment. Your therapist is building a picture of how your symptoms developed, what makes them worse, what helps even a little. This part feels slow when you're in pain. I always tell people to tolerate it, because a good assessment leads to a better treatment plan, and a better plan means fewer wasted months on approaches that don't fit.

Around sessions four through eight, you'll typically start getting into the actual work: identifying thought patterns, building skills, making behavioral changes in small increments. This phase often feels harder before it feels better. That's not a failure, it's what exposure to avoided things feels like initially.

By session ten or twelve, most people start noticing something shift. Not a dramatic turnaround, usually. More like: "I had a bad week but I didn't spiral for as long." Or: "I went to that thing I'd been avoiding and it was fine." Small data points that accumulate.

Some people will also be working with a psychiatrist on medication during this period, which is worth addressing directly. Therapy and medication are not in competition. For moderate to severe presentations of both depression and anxiety, the research consistently shows better outcomes with a combination approach than either alone. That's not a knock on therapy; it's just what the data says.

## You Don't Have to Have It All Figured Out Before You Start

A lot of people delay starting therapy because they want to be able to articulate exactly what's wrong. They want to show up prepared, to not waste the therapist's time.

You don't have to do that.

You can show up and say, "I feel anxious and depressed and I don't really know which is which or why, and I've been putting this off for a year." That is a complete and valid starting point. The therapist's job is to help you make sense of it, not to receive a well-organized report.

The harder obstacle, honestly, is the one depression specifically creates: the belief that it won't work, that you're too far gone, that you've tried before and it didn't help. I've heard this from so many people, and I never dismiss it, because sometimes previous therapy genuinely wasn't the right fit or the right approach. But I also know that giving up after one mismatch is like deciding restaurants aren't for you because one gave you food poisoning.

If you've had therapy before and it didn't help, tell your new therapist that. Tell them specifically what felt unhelpful. That information is useful diagnostic data, not a complaint.

### FAQ

#### Can therapy alone treat depression and anxiety, or do I need medication too?

Therapy alone can be very effective, especially for mild to moderate depression and anxiety. For more severe presentations, or when symptoms are making it hard to engage with therapy at all, medication may help enough to make the therapy work. Talk to both a therapist and a psychiatrist or your primary care doctor before deciding.

#### How do I know if my anxiety is separate from my depression or just part of it?

Honestly, the distinction matters less than you might think when it comes to treatment. Some anxiety is a feature of depression itself, some is a separate anxiety disorder, and a competent therapist will assess this over time. You don't need to know before you start.

#### What if I can't afford regular therapy right now?

Sliding-scale clinics, community mental health centers, and training clinics at universities (where supervised graduate students provide therapy at reduced cost) are real options. Open Path Collective offers sessions from $30 to $80. Online tools like CBT-based apps can supplement care, though they shouldn't replace it for significant symptoms.

#### How long before I start feeling better in therapy?

Most people notice some improvement within six to eight weeks, though this varies widely by severity and how well the approach fits. If you've had several sessions and feel no movement at all, it's worth discussing a shift in approach with your therapist rather than assuming therapy itself isn't working.

#### Is it normal to feel worse when I first start therapy?

Yes, and more often than people expect. Early sessions can stir up things you've been avoiding, and the work of changing thought patterns and behaviors is genuinely uncomfortable at first. Feeling worse in the first few weeks isn't a sign to stop; it's usually a sign something is actually being addressed.
