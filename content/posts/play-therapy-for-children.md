Most parents who ask me about play therapy are expecting me to talk them into it. They've already Googled it, watched a few reassuring YouTube videos, and arrived at my inbox half-convinced but unsure if it's "real" therapy or just an expensive playdate. Here's what I actually tell them: it's one of the most evidence-backed approaches we have for children under twelve. The fact that it looks like playing? That's a feature, not a flaw.

Let me explain why.

## What Play Therapy Actually Is (And Isn't)

Kids don't process trauma, anxiety, or grief by sitting in a chair and talking through feelings for fifty minutes. Their brains aren't built that way yet. The prefrontal cortex, the part that handles verbal reasoning and self-reflection, is still very much under construction. Play is how children think. It's how they test ideas, rehearse fears, and make sense of confusing experiences.

Play therapy takes that developmental reality seriously. A trained play therapist creates a structured environment, usually a dedicated playroom stocked with specific materials, where children can express and process what they can't yet put into words. Sand trays. Puppets. Art supplies. Dollhouses. Foam weapons for safe aggression release. None of this is accidental. Every item is chosen deliberately, and the therapist is doing active clinical work the whole time.

There are two broad approaches. In non-directive (or child-centered) play therapy, the child leads and the therapist follows, reflects, and creates safety. Virginia Axline developed this model in the 1940s and it's been refined ever since. It gives children control they often lack elsewhere. The directive model is more structured: the therapist introduces specific activities or narratives to work toward a particular goal. Most clinicians use both, depending on what the child needs that week.

What it isn't: babysitting. A real play therapist has at minimum a master's degree in counseling or social work, specialized training in play therapy techniques, and often a Registered Play Therapist (RPT) credential through the Association for Play Therapy (APT). If someone calls themselves a play therapist without that background, ask questions.

## Who It's Actually For

Play therapy works for a wider range of kids than most parents realize.

The strongest research supports it for children ages three to twelve. A 2001 meta-analysis published in the *International Journal of Play Therapy* (Ray et al., covering 94 studies) found effect sizes comparable to those seen in adult CBT research. More recent work has continued supporting its effectiveness for anxiety, ADHD, trauma, attachment difficulties, grief, and adjustment issues like divorce or a new sibling.

Adolescents can benefit too, though by thirteen or fourteen most teens do better with talk therapy or modalities like DBT. Don't rule it out for a mature eleven-year-old who still plays enthusiastically, and don't force it on a twelve-year-old who'd find it embarrassing.

Some parents bring kids in for behavioral issues: hitting at school, tantrums way past the developmental norm, defiance that's escalating. Play therapy can help, but I'd encourage a full evaluation first to rule out sensory processing issues, ADHD, or learning differences driving the behavior. Play therapy and a diagnostic workup aren't mutually exclusive.

Children who've experienced trauma, especially complex or developmental trauma, often do exceptionally well with Trauma-Focused Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (TF-CBT), which incorporates structured play and expressive activities. If your child has a trauma history, ask specifically whether a potential therapist is TF-CBT trained. The APT's [therapist finder](https://www.a4pt.org/search/custom.asp?id=1955) lets you filter by specialty.

## What to Expect, Session by Session

The first one or two sessions typically involve the parents more than the child. The therapist takes a developmental history, asks about the concerns that prompted the referral, and explains their approach. Some therapists meet with children alone from day one; others want a brief parent-child intro. Both are fine.

Then the actual work starts. It often looks frustratingly low-key from the outside.

Early sessions, children are usually exploring the space and testing limits: what can I do here, what happens if I make a mess, will this adult stay calm if I get loud? That's not wasted time. The therapist is building the relationship that makes the real work possible. Don't be surprised if your child comes out of the first three sessions seemingly totally fine and reporting they just "played." They probably did. They're also building trust.

Around sessions four through eight, depending on the child, you'll often see a shift. Parents sometimes describe a temporary increase in difficult behavior at home. The therapist will likely have warned you about this. The child is starting to process things, and that process isn't linear or neat. Stick with it.

Progress in play therapy isn't always visible the way it is with a medication trial. You're looking for gradual shifts: fewer meltdowns over weeks, better sleep, more flexible thinking, an ability to name feelings that wasn't there before. Most courses of play therapy run between sixteen and thirty sessions, though acute issues can resolve faster and complex trauma takes longer. Weekly sessions are standard.

Parent consultations, where the therapist meets with you briefly without your child, are a normal part of the process. These matter. Ask for them if your therapist doesn't offer them proactively.

## The Cost Question (Honest Version)

Sessions typically run $100 to $200 depending on region and the therapist's credentials. Many insurance plans cover it under mental health benefits, but coverage varies wildly. Some insurers will only reimburse if there's a diagnosable condition listed in the DSM-5. That means your child may need a diagnosis like Adjustment Disorder, Anxiety Disorder, or PTSD on file for sessions to be covered. Ask the therapist's billing contact before your first appointment.

Community mental health centers often offer sliding-scale fees. SAMHSA's treatment locator at [findtreatment.gov](https://findtreatment.gov/) can help you find providers in your area with financial assistance options.

If your family is in crisis or you're worried about a child's safety, don't wait on a therapy waitlist. The [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/) serves families and young people, not just adults.

## Supporting the Work at Home

You don't have to stay out of it. Some of the most effective play therapy I've seen involves parents who do their own reading and make small changes at home that reinforce what's happening in sessions.

If your child's therapist uses an expressive or feelings-based approach, a simple feelings journal or a workbook like *A Terrible Thing Happened* by Margaret M. Holmes can be genuinely helpful at home for kids processing scary events. For parents wanting to understand the theory better, *The Whole-Brain Child* by Daniel J. Siegel and Tina Payne Bryson is worth your time (affiliate link: the site may earn a small commission if you purchase through our links).

Neither replaces therapy, but both extend the work. Ask the therapist what you can do. A good one will tell you.
