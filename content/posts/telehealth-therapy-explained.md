You've been meaning to call a therapist for months. Finally, you do. The first three aren't taking new patients. The fourth is 45 minutes away with only Tuesday morning appointments. You work Tuesday mornings. Browser closes. You'll try again later. For most people, later never happens. Telehealth therapy exists because of exactly this gap, between deciding you need help and actually getting it.

## What Telehealth Therapy Actually Is

Telehealth therapy is real mental health treatment from a licensed professional delivered through video, phone, or secure messaging instead of an office visit. Simple enough. But there's more to it.

The person you're talking to has credentials: a licensed clinical social worker (LCSW), licensed professional counselor (LPC), licensed marriage and family therapist (LMFT), psychologist (PhD or PsyD), or psychiatrist (MD or DO). Same training. Same ethics rules. Often the same supervision requirements as someone in a traditional office. The medium changes. The qualifications don't.

Video is most common and mirrors in-person therapy most closely. Phone sessions work for people without solid internet or those who just prefer it. Some platforms offer text-based therapy where you message back and forth with your therapist over days. Clinicians debate this last one because real-time connection drops off, but it works for certain people pursuing specific goals.

I've watched skeptical clients become video therapy's biggest fans after a few sessions. The doubt is usually whether anything real happens through a screen. It does. The therapeutic relationship, one of the strongest predictors of whether therapy actually works, forms just as meaningfully on video. A 2018 meta-analysis in the *Journal of Affective Disorders* found telehealth cognitive behavioral therapy produced outcomes matching in-person CBT for depression and anxiety. That's a significant finding.

Telehealth isn't automatically better than in-person therapy, but it's genuinely right for a broad range of people.

People with physical disabilities or chronic illness who can't easily travel. People in rural areas where a good therapist doesn't exist or lives an hour away. Parents of young kids who can't carve out travel time. People with social anxiety who dread waiting rooms. Shift workers with chaotic schedules. People who travel constantly for work. Telehealth becomes meaningfully more accessible for all of them.

There's another group worth naming directly: people trying therapy for the first time and carrying shame about it. For some, the privacy of being home, not walking into an office building someone might recognize, lowers the barrier enough to actually show up. That matters more than it sounds.

Where it falls short: people in active psychosis, with severe eating disorders needing medical oversight, or actively suicidal typically need in-person or higher-level care. If you're in immediate danger, call or text the [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/) at 988. It's available 24/7 and built exactly for moments when telehealth isn't enough.

Most therapy types work across telehealth. Cognitive behavioral therapy translates beautifully to video. If you want background on how it works, our guide on [cognitive behavioral therapy explained](/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-explained/) covers the fundamentals. DBT skills groups have shifted online successfully. Even [EMDR therapy](/emdr-therapy-explained/), which many assumed required in-person work, adapts well to telehealth with the right tools and training.

## How to Get Started: A Practical Step-by-Step Guide

Getting into telehealth therapy is simpler than most people think. Here's how you move from contemplating it to actually doing it.

**Step 1: Identify what you're looking to address.**
You don't need a diagnosis or a perfectly worded problem. A rough sense helps, whether it's anxiety, relationship trouble, grief, or just persistent wrongness. It helps you find someone with actual experience in what you're dealing with.

**Step 2: Check your insurance.**
Coverage expanded significantly after 2020. Many plans now cover telehealth at the same rate as in-person visits. Our guide on [whether insurance covers therapy](/does-insurance-cover-therapy/) explains what to look for. Call your insurance's member services number and ask directly: "Does my plan cover telehealth therapy with a licensed mental health provider?"

**Step 3: Search for providers.**
SAMHSA's [treatment locator at findtreatment.gov](https://findtreatment.gov/) is free and government-maintained. It helps you find licensed providers by location and specialty, including those offering telehealth. Psychology Today's therapist finder has a telehealth filter too. Our article on [how to find the right therapist](/how-to-find-the-right-therapist/) covers additional factors beyond just credentials.

**Step 4: Book a consultation.**
Most therapists offer a free 15 to 20-minute phone or video consultation. Use it. Ask about their experience with your specific issue, their theoretical approach, and their openness. Fit matters as much as credentials.

**Step 5: Set up your space.**
Find somewhere private where interruptions won't happen. Use headphones if you're around other people. A stable internet connection: aim for at least 10 Mbps upload speed. Have a backup plan (your phone's data) in case Wi-Fi drops during a session.

**Step 6: Show up for the first session.**
It'll probably feel awkward. That's normal. Give it at least three sessions before deciding whether it's working.

## Telehealth vs. In-Person Therapy: An Honest Comparison

Neither is universally better. Here's what actually differs:

| Factor | Telehealth | In-Person |
|---|---|---|
| Accessibility | High, no travel required | Limited by geography and transportation |
| Privacy | Variable, depends on your home | Typically higher in a dedicated office |
| Nonverbal cues | Partial, body language partially visible | Full, including posture and movement |
| Crisis response | Limited, provider can contact emergency services but can't be physically present | More direct options available |
| Scheduling flexibility | Usually better | Often more rigid |
| Technology barriers | Real concern for some | Not an issue |
| Insurance coverage | Increasingly comparable | Broadly covered |
| Modality availability | Most modalities work; some limitations with somatic therapies | Full range available |

The biggest practical difference I've noticed: people in difficult home environments, whether a controlling partner, noisy shared housing, or zero privacy, genuinely struggle with telehealth. That's not a format failure. It's a logistics problem worth solving before you start, not after.

## What to Expect in a Telehealth Session

A telehealth session follows nearly the same structure as in-person therapy. Sessions typically run 45 to 55 minutes. The first session or two is usually intake, where your therapist collects background, discusses goals, and explains how they work.

After that, sessions vary by therapist and your needs. A CBT therapist might work through thought records or behavioral activation with you on screen. Someone using [Acceptance and Commitment Therapy](/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy-act/) might guide you through values work. A DBT-trained therapist usually assigns skills practice between sessions. Homework doesn't disappear because you're at home.

Some clients find it helpful to supplement sessions with structured self-reflection between appointments. A CBT journal or mindfulness workbook can reinforce what you're working on in therapy. David Burns's *Feeling Good* workbook or a dedicated thought record journal makes the work between sessions more tangible. (Disclosure: this site may earn a commission on Amazon purchases.)

Confidentiality works exactly the same online. Your therapist is legally and ethically required to protect your information, and legitimate telehealth platforms are HIPAA-compliant. If a platform isn't HIPAA-compliant, ask about it before you share personal details.

## Understanding Costs and Insurance for Telehealth

Cost stops a lot of people from getting therapy entirely. Telehealth doesn't automatically cost less, but it opens up options.

Many private plans now cover telehealth mental health services at parity with in-person sessions, especially after pandemic-era legislation. Medicaid coverage for telehealth varies by state. Medicare covers telehealth therapy for its beneficiaries. For a detailed breakdown of what different insurance types cover and what to ask your insurer, our guide on [finding a therapist that takes insurance](/finding-a-therapist-that-takes-insurance/) is practical and concrete.

If you're uninsured or underinsured, community mental health centers often offer telehealth on a sliding scale. Some university training clinics now run telehealth with lower-cost sessions supervised by experienced clinicians. Open Path Collective is a nonprofit network offering reduced fees specifically for underinsured people.

For a full look at how therapy costs are structured across different payment types, our article on [how much therapy costs](/how-much-does-therapy-cost/) covers the range without sugarcoating what most people actually pay.
