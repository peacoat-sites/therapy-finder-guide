On November 9, 2026, Compass Pathways finished submitting its New Drug Application for COMP360, a synthesized psilocybin therapy designed for treatment-resistant depression. That alone would be news. But the company also holds an FDA Commissioner's National Priority Voucher, which could compress the standard review timeline, potentially allowing a faster review than the standard ten to twelve months. That means a federally approved psilocybin therapy could be a clinical reality by early 2027. For the millions of Americans whose depression hasn't responded to conventional treatment, this is not an abstract regulatory story. It's a question they may need to answer soon: if this gets approved, can I actually afford it?

The short answer, [right now](/the-va-is-testing-mdma-assisted-therapy-for-ptsd-right-now/), is probably not through insurance. And even after approval, the path to coverage will be complicated. Compass CFO Englebert has said publicly that insurers will likely block coverage initially while they assess the product, a pattern familiar to anyone who watched the rollout of SPRAVATO (esketamine), the nasal-spray depression drug that required years of insurer negotiation before coverage became reasonably accessible. Psilocybin is likely to follow a similar arc, and understanding that arc now gives patients, families, and clinicians a head start.

According to Psychedelic Alpha's November 20, 2026 coverage, the question of who pays for [psychedelic therapy](/what-the-new-va-hhs-psychedelic-therapy-mou-means-for-therapists/) is becoming urgent precisely because approval could arrive before any coverage framework is in place. Compass has said it's preparing patient support and affordability programs, but the specifics haven't been announced. What we know is what people are paying out-of-pocket right now in state-legal markets: between $500 and $3,400 per session, with zero insurance reimbursement available.

## Why COMP360 Is Different From What You've Heard Before

There's been a lot of psilocybin hype over the past decade. Clinical trials stalled, state ballot measures created a patchwork of semi-legal access, and the FDA's 2024 rejection of MDMA-[assisted therapy](/the-va-is-testing-mdma-assisted-therapy-for-ptsd-right-now/) for PTSD put a chill on the entire space. COMP360 is different in a specific, clinical sense: it has completed two Phase 3 randomized controlled trials, COMP005 (results reported June 2025) and COMP006 (February 2026), and hit its primary endpoints in both. That's the clinical bar the FDA needs cleared. As Psychedelic Beacon's approval tracker notes, this makes COMP360 the most clinically advanced classic psychedelic therapy in the U.S. pipeline, full stop.

The expected DEA scheduling outcome, Schedule III, matters for coverage too. Schedule III substances, which include things like ketamine and anabolic steroids for medical use, can be prescribed and dispensed in clinical settings. SPRAVATO, as an esketamine product, operates under a similar framework with an FDA Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) program requiring in-clinic administration and monitoring. Jefferies analyst Andrew Tsai confirmed the November 9 NDA completion date and noted that Schedule III is Compass's anticipated outcome, which would at least remove the federal legal barrier that currently makes insurance coverage impossible to structure.

## What Insurance Coverage Could Actually Look Like

Here's where the SPRAVATO comparison gets instructive and a little sobering. Esketamine received FDA approval in March 2019. Insurance coverage didn't become broadly available until years later, and even now it typically requires prior authorization, documented failure on multiple antidepressants, and in-office administration with post-dose monitoring. Many patients still pay hundreds of dollars per session even with coverage.

Psilocybin therapy adds layers SPRAVATO doesn't have. Sessions are longer, often six to eight hours. They typically involve a trained therapist present throughout. That therapist time is a separate billing question from the drug itself, one that existing insurance billing codes weren't designed to handle. The Psychedelic Alpha analysis from November 20, 2026 flags this explicitly: the cost of the therapy component and the cost of the medicine may need to be billed separately, and payers have no current framework for the former.

A rough look at the cost landscape helps clarify what patients might face at different access points:

| Access Type | Estimated Cost Per Session | Insurance Coverage |
|---|---|---|
| Current state-legal psilocybin (e.g., Oregon) | $500 to $3,400 | None |
| SPRAVATO (esketamine) with insurance | $10 to $100+ copay (varies) | Yes, with prior auth |
| SPRAVATO without insurance | $700 to $900 per session | None |
| COMP360 post-approval (projected) | Unknown; likely $1,000+ until coverage | None initially |

Compass has signaled it will offer patient assistance programs, the kind of affordability bridge that drug manufacturers often deploy in the gap between approval and widespread coverage. Those programs typically help people below certain income thresholds, but they're not a permanent solution for the middle-income patient who earns too much to qualify and too little to comfortably pay out-of-pocket.

## The Broader Pipeline Changes the Negotiating Dynamic

COMP360 isn't the only psychedelic therapy knocking on the FDA's door. In April 2026, the FDA issued Commissioner's National Priority Vouchers to both Usona Institute, which is studying psilocybin for major depressive disorder, and Transcend Therapeutics, which is researching a psychedelic-assisted therapy for PTSD. That's three separate psychedelic-adjacent therapies potentially approaching approval within overlapping timeframes.

This matters for coverage negotiations in a way that a single drug approval wouldn't. When insurers are facing a single new drug, they can delay and assess indefinitely. When a category of treatments is incoming, with multiple manufacturers and clinical indications, the pressure to develop a reimbursement framework becomes harder to defer. Advocacy organizations, employers offering mental health benefits, and state Medicaid programs are all going to need a policy position, and that policy conversation is effectively starting now, in November 2026, not after approval.

The Psychedelics Today piece from November 4, 2026 put it well: the access and coverage problems that follow approval are problems the field will be lucky to have, because they're only possible if the clinical and regulatory work succeeds. That framing is useful. These aren't reasons to dismiss psilocybin therapy's potential. They're the real-world logistics that determine whether that potential reaches actual patients.

## What Patients With Treatment-Resistant Depression Should Do Right Now

If you have treatment-resistant depression and you're watching this story, the most grounded thing you can do is get current with a psychiatrist or prescribing clinician who knows your history. That relationship is what creates the documented treatment record, failed antidepressants, failed augmentation strategies, prior authorizations, that insurance companies will almost certainly require before they cover any new therapy.

If you're newer to exploring options and not sure where to start, understanding which therapy approaches fit your situation is worth doing before any specific treatment is on the table. This quick quiz on [which therapy type fits you](/which-therapy-type/) can help orient you before you walk into a first appointment.

For people in state-legal markets like Oregon or Colorado where facilitated psilocybin services already exist, some access is available now, but costs are entirely out-of-pocket and the regulatory and clinical structures differ from what an FDA-approved drug pathway would look like. That distinction matters. State-legal facilitated services and an FDA-approved prescription medication are not the same product, even if they use the same molecule.

This is a genuinely unusual moment in mental health treatment. A therapy that was classified as having no accepted medical use for over fifty years is days or weeks away from federal approval review. The science got here faster than the coverage infrastructure. That gap is real, and it's worth taking seriously before the headlines move on.
