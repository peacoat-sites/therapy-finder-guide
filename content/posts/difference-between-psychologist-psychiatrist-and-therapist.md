Most people spend more time researching a new laptop than they do figuring out who to actually call when they're struggling. That's not a criticism. It's just what happens when the system is this confusing and the stakes feel this high.

If you've typed something like "do I need a therapist or a psychiatrist" into a search bar recently, you're in good company. I've heard some version of that question hundreds of times, from people sitting in my office, from readers who email me, from friends who pull me aside at parties because they don't know who else to ask. The titles sound interchangeable until you need to know the difference. Then it matters a lot.

So let me just lay it out plainly.

## The Shortest Possible Version (Before We Go Deeper)

A **psychiatrist** is a medical doctor who specializes in mental health. They went to medical school, completed a residency in psychiatry, and are licensed to prescribe medication.

A **psychologist** typically has a doctoral degree (a PhD or PsyD) in psychology. They're trained extensively in psychological testing and assessment, and in most U.S. states, they cannot prescribe medication (there are a few exceptions, like Louisiana and New Mexico, where licensed psychologists with additional training can prescribe).

A **therapist** is a broader term that covers a range of licensed professionals: licensed clinical social workers (LCSWs), licensed professional counselors (LPCs), licensed marriage and family therapists (LMFTs), and yes, psychologists and psychiatrists can do therapy too. What ties the "therapist" category together is the work: talk-based treatment, the kind of thing that happens week after week in a room or on a video call.

Here's what I tell people who are just getting oriented: the word "therapist" describes what someone does. The words "psychologist" and "psychiatrist" describe their training and credentials.

## What Psychiatrists Actually Do (And When You Should See One)

Honestly, a lot of people picture a psychiatrist as someone who just hands out prescriptions in 15-minute appointments. That image isn't completely wrong, but it's a significant oversimplification.

Psychiatrists are the people you want in the room for anything that has a strong biological component. Schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, severe treatment-resistant depression, certain presentations of OCD, situations where someone has tried therapy and isn't getting better. They understand the pharmacology. They know how medications interact. They can order labs, interpret results, and see mental health through the lens of medicine, which genuinely matters in ways that aren't always obvious.

You might be wondering: "So do psychiatrists do therapy?" Some do. Fewer than you'd think. Many psychiatrists now focus almost entirely on medication management, partly because of insurance incentives and partly because there aren't enough providers to go around. If you see a psychiatrist for ongoing care, you'll often also be referred to a separate therapist for the talk-therapy component. That split model is extremely common and, when coordinated well, can work really effectively.

Cost is real and I won't pretend otherwise. Psychiatrist appointments frequently run $200 to $500 or more without insurance, and many psychiatrists are out-of-network providers. If cost is a barrier, community mental health centers and federally qualified health centers (FQHCs) often have psychiatrists on staff who see patients on a sliding scale.

## What Psychologists Bring to the Table

The training distinction here matters more than most people realize. To become a licensed psychologist, you typically complete a four-to-seven-year doctoral program plus a supervised internship. That's a lot of time doing one thing: understanding how people think, feel, and behave, and learning how to assess and treat problems in those areas.

Psychologists are particularly well-suited for psychological testing. If someone needs a formal evaluation for ADHD, a learning disability, autism spectrum disorder, or a complex diagnostic picture, a psychologist with specialization in assessment is often the right person to call. That testing isn't cheap ($1,500 to $4,000 for a comprehensive neuropsychological battery, depending on the provider and region) but it can answer questions that years of guesswork won't.

Psychologists also do a lot of therapy, and in many settings, they're trained in specialized evidence-based treatments like prolonged exposure therapy for PTSD, dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), and intensive CBT protocols. The research on these approaches is pretty solid, not perfect, but solid.

One thing that confuses people: the title "psychologist" is legally protected in all 50 states. A "therapist" or "counselor" is not always. More on that in a second.

## The Therapist Umbrella: Who's Under It and Why It's Not a Downgrade

I want to address something directly because I've seen it cause real harm. There's a quiet assumption floating around that a therapist is somehow less than a psychologist or psychiatrist, a consolation prize when you can't access the "real" professionals. That assumption is wrong.

LCSWs, LPCs, and LMFTs do the majority of outpatient talk therapy in the United States. They're trained specifically in clinical work. A skilled LCSW with 15 years of experience treating anxiety is not a lesser option than a freshly licensed psychologist. For most people dealing with depression, anxiety, relationship stress, grief, life transitions, trauma, or burnout, a licensed therapist is exactly the right level of care.

The credential requirements vary by state, but generally these professionals have master's degrees, thousands of hours of supervised clinical experience, and have passed licensing exams. The [National Alliance on Mental Illness (NAMI)](https://www.nami.org/) has a really useful overview of provider types if you want to go deeper on the specific licensing differences in your state.

Where I would pump the brakes is with anyone using the word "therapist" or "life coach" without a license attached. In many states, those titles are unregulated. That's not automatically disqualifying, coaches can be helpful in specific contexts, but if you're dealing with a diagnosable mental health condition, please see someone who is licensed and carries liability insurance and is bound by an ethical code. The stakes are too high for ambiguity.

## How to Actually Choose

You might be wondering where to even start. Here's my honest, practical answer: start with what's bothering you and whether medication is something you're open to or already know you need.

If you're experiencing symptoms that feel biological (sleep is completely disrupted, mood is cycling in ways that feel outside your control, you're having thoughts that scare you, previous therapy alone hasn't worked) a psychiatrist is probably the right first call. Your primary care doctor can also be a bridge here. Many PCPs are comfortable prescribing antidepressants for mild to moderate depression and anxiety, and they can refer you to a psychiatrist for anything more complex.

If you want to talk through patterns, process something that happened, work on relationships, manage anxiety or depression, or just get better at being a person, a therapist is where to start. [Psychology Today's therapist directory](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/therapists) lets you filter by insurance, specialty, location, and the type of therapy offered, and it's one of the more genuinely useful tools for finding someone in your area.

If you're dealing with something that needs careful psychological assessment (a new ADHD diagnosis, figuring out whether a child has a learning disability, sorting out a complicated diagnostic picture), a psychologist with testing specialization is your person.

These categories overlap, and that's okay. A lot of people end up with a therapist they see weekly and a psychiatrist they check in with every month or two for medication management. That combination isn't unusual. It's often actually the most effective arrangement.

A few books I often point people to when they're starting to build their self-understanding before or alongside therapy: *The Anxiety and Worry Workbook* by Clark and Beck is one of the most practical CBT-based tools I've encountered, and *The DBT Skills Workbook* by McKay, Wood, and Brantley is excellent if emotional regulation is a challenge. (Both are available on Amazon, and the site may earn a small commission if you purchase through links here.) These won't replace working with a professional, but they give you something to do between sessions and help you show up to therapy with more clarity about what you're working on.
