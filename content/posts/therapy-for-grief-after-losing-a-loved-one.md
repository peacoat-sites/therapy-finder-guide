Grief doesn't follow a schedule. And if you're reading this, you're probably somewhere in the thick of it, maybe weeks out from a loss, maybe years, wondering why it still hits you sideways when you open a cabinet and see their coffee mug. You might be wondering whether what you're feeling is "normal," or whether it's finally time to talk to someone. Both questions deserve real consideration.

Here's what I tell people at this stage: there's no wrong time to seek support. But there is a genuine difference between the grief that moves through you, even painfully, and the grief that starts to harden, the kind that quietly wrecks your sleep, your relationships, your ability to imagine any future at all. Therapy can help with both. The specifics just depend on where you're standing.

## What Grief Therapy Actually Is (and Isn't)

A lot of people come in expecting a therapist to help them "get over it." Drop that framing immediately. The goal of grief therapy isn't to stop loving someone or to stop missing them. It's to find a way to carry the loss without being crushed by it.

Grief therapy is a specific application of talk therapy, usually drawing on several different treatment approaches depending on what's happening for a particular person. It's not crisis counseling. It's not just venting into a professionally licensed void. A skilled grief therapist will help you understand what's happening emotionally, identify where you're stuck, work through things like guilt or anger or relief (yes, relief, that one comes up more than people admit), and eventually help you figure out what life looks like now.

Here's something I want to be honest about: not every therapist who lists "grief" on their profile has serious training in it. When you're looking for someone, ask directly whether they work with bereavement regularly and what approaches they use. You deserve an actual answer, not a vague reassurance.

## The Types of Therapy That Actually Help

This is where things get a little technical, but it matters because knowing the difference genuinely helps you find the right fit.

**Grief-Focused CBT (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy)** targets the thoughts and behaviors that have wrapped themselves around the loss. If you've started avoiding places, stopped doing things you used to love, or find yourself spinning in "I should have known" or "I could have done more," that's exactly what CBT covers. It's structured. There's homework. Some people love that; others find it too clinical right after a major loss. A 2021 meta-analysis in *Clinical Psychology Review* found CBT-based approaches significantly reduced complicated grief symptoms, which is worth knowing if you're considering it.

**Complicated Grief Treatment (CGT)**, developed by Dr. M. Katherine Shear at Columbia University, is a specific 16-session protocol designed for what clinicians now call "prolonged grief disorder." This is grief that hasn't budged, even after a year or more, and that's seriously impairing daily functioning. If you feel genuinely stuck, not just sad but unable to accept the reality of the death at some level, or feeling like life has no point without this person, CGT might be the most targeted option available. It's not widely offered everywhere, but the Columbia Center for Complicated Grief maintains a directory of trained clinicians.

**Narrative therapy** gets used less in grief work than it should. The basic idea is that you're the author of your life's story, and a loss feels like the story got violently rewritten without your permission. Narrative therapy helps you re-engage with authorship, not by pretending the loss didn't happen, but by finding where the person you lost still lives in who you are.

**EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing)** sounds strange if you haven't run into it before. I remember being skeptical when a colleague first described it to me. But when grief is tangled up with trauma, a sudden or violent death, a prolonged illness you witnessed, an accident, EMDR has a solid evidence base for processing the traumatic elements specifically. It's not the fit for every grief situation, but when grief carries that traumatic edge, it can do things talk therapy alone sometimes can't.

Group therapy and support groups deserve mention here too, though they're not the same thing. A grief support group (often peer-led, like those run through hospices or community centers) offers connection and the huge relief of normalization. Grief group therapy, led by a licensed therapist, adds clinical structure. For some people, hearing someone else say out loud the exact thing they were ashamed to think is worth more than months of one-on-one sessions. These two work well together.

## Prolonged Grief: When to Take It More Seriously

Grief is supposed to hurt. I need to be clear about that, because there's an unfortunate cultural habit of pathologizing sadness too quickly and, paradoxically, of telling people to "move on" before they're ready.

But prolonged grief disorder, formally recognized in the DSM-5-TR in 2022, is real, and it affects roughly 10% of bereaved people. The markers aren't just "still sad after a year." They include intense longing that doesn't ease, difficulty accepting the death, feeling like part of yourself died, inability to trust others since the loss, bitterness or anger that hasn't softened, and real interference with daily functioning. If those phrases describe what's been happening for you, don't wait longer to find a therapist. The [National Alliance on Mental Illness (NAMI)](https://www.nami.org/) has a helpline at 1-800-950-NAMI and resources specifically for navigating prolonged and complex grief.

There's also an important intersection with suicidal thinking. Grief, especially after a traumatic or unexpected loss, can sometimes go to very dark places. If you're having thoughts of not wanting to be here, the [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/) is available 24/7 by call or text. Grief that touches that territory needs professional support, not just time.

## The Practical Questions: Finding Someone and Paying for It

So you've decided you want to try therapy. Here's where a lot of people stall out.

Finding a grief therapist specifically: Psychology Today's therapist directory lets you filter by specialty, including "grief." The Association for Death Education and Counseling (ADEC) maintains a directory of certified grief counselors at adec.org. Your primary care doctor can give referrals too, and hospice organizations in your area often offer free or low-cost bereavement counseling to anyone in the community, not just families of their patients. That last option is hugely underused.

Cost is real and I won't pretend otherwise. Individual therapy in most U.S. cities runs roughly $120-$250 per session without insurance. Many therapists offer sliding scale fees; asking is completely reasonable. Community mental health centers charge on income-based scales. If you have insurance, mental health benefits have improved significantly since the Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act, though navigating the insurance paperwork can be exhausting when you're already depleted. Open Path Collective (openpathcollective.org) is a network where therapists offer sessions at $30-$80 for people who qualify.

Between sessions, some people find structured workbooks genuinely useful as a complement to therapy (not a replacement). [The Grief Recovery Handbook by John W. James and Russell Friedman](https://www.amazon.com/Grief-Recovery-Handbook-20th-Anniversary/dp/0061686077?tag=contentportfo-20) has endured for decades for good reason. There are also CBT-based grief journals that can help you track thoughts and notice patterns week to week. *(As an Amazon Associate this site earns from qualifying purchases.)*

## What to Expect in the First Few Sessions

People are often surprised that the first session isn't as hard as they feared, then surprised again when session three or four hits harder than expected. That's normal.

Early sessions are mostly history: the relationship, the circumstances of the death, what the loss has felt like, what's been working and what hasn't. A good therapist won't push you to process more than you're ready to handle in the first meeting. They're also assessing what kind of grief this is and what approaches might fit.

You might feel emotionally wrung out after sessions, especially early ones. Build in buffer time afterward if you can. Don't schedule a major work meeting right after your first few appointments.

You also have every right to change therapists if something isn't clicking by session four or five. The relationship between you and a therapist matters for outcomes, the research is consistent on this. A therapist who specializes in grief but doesn't feel like a good fit for you specifically isn't the right therapist.
