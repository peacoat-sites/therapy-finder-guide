The honest answer nobody wants to hear is: you probably can't know which one is right for you without trying. And that uncertainty is genuinely hard to sit with when you're depressed and barely have the energy to make coffee in the morning, let alone research treatment options.

But here's what I've seen, working alongside psychiatrists, therapists, and the people caught between them: the "therapy vs. medication" framing is itself part of the problem. It forces a competition where there doesn't need to be one. So before we get into the real differences (and there are meaningful ones, worth knowing), I want to say clearly: most of the people I've watched get genuinely better used both, at least for a period. That doesn't mean you have to. It just means the either/or question may be leading you somewhere less useful than you think.

That said, you came here for actual information, so let's get into it.

## What the research actually says

There's a 2016 study published in *World Psychiatry* that summarized decades of data on this, and the finding was pretty consistent: for mild to moderate depression, psychotherapy (particularly cognitive behavioral therapy, or CBT) and antidepressants produce roughly comparable outcomes. Neither crushes the other. For severe depression, the evidence tilts toward medication, often in combination with therapy, because severe depression can impair the concentration and emotional processing that therapy requires.

What most people don't realize is that response rates for antidepressants alone, at the individual level, are humbling. The STAR*D trial, a massive NIMH-funded study from the early 2000s, found that only about a third of patients achieved remission on their first antidepressant. A third. That means two-thirds of people had to try something different, either a different medication, a higher dose, or adding something else entirely. That's not a condemnation of medication. It's just the honest picture.

Therapy's success rates have their own asterisks. CBT works well for many people, but it requires engagement. You have to do the homework, challenge the thoughts, show up even when it feels pointless. For someone in a severe depressive episode, that cognitive load can be a real barrier. I've talked to people who dropped out of therapy not because it was wrong for them, but because they needed to be stabilized on medication first before they had enough mental bandwidth to actually benefit from sessions.

The research here is genuinely mixed on long-term outcomes. Some studies suggest therapy has a more durable effect after treatment ends (because you've built skills you keep using), while medication effects tend to taper when you stop taking it. But this isn't universal, and it doesn't mean medication is just a band-aid. For some people, a course of antidepressants during a hard period is exactly what they needed, and they move on. For others, long-term maintenance medication is the appropriate, well-supported choice.

## What therapy actually does (and doesn't do)

Therapy is not just talking about your feelings. I say this because that misconception stops a lot of people, particularly men, from ever starting. CBT, which is the most researched approach for depression, is structured, somewhat skills-based, and very focused on the relationship between your thoughts, behaviors, and mood. A good CBT therapist will assign you homework. They'll ask you to track your thoughts, test assumptions, schedule activities that counteract withdrawal. It can feel almost clinical.

Other modalities are worth knowing: Behavioral Activation is a stripped-down approach that focuses specifically on getting you moving and re-engaging with life (strong evidence base, underrated). IPT (Interpersonal Therapy) works well when depression is tied to grief, role transitions, or relationship conflict. ACT (Acceptance and Commitment Therapy) takes a different angle entirely, less about changing thoughts, more about changing your relationship to them.

What therapy won't do is fix a chemical imbalance quickly. And the "chemical imbalance" framing is itself contested now (the serotonin theory of depression is less solid than pharmaceutical marketing made it seem for decades), but the underlying point stands: if there's a strong biological component to your depression, whether genetic, hormonal, or neurological, therapy alone may not be sufficient. That's not a failure of therapy. It's just biology being stubborn.

Cost is real and I won't pretend otherwise. A weekly therapy session in a major city runs $150 to $300 out of pocket. With insurance it can be much lower, but insurance coverage for mental health remains inconsistent and frustrating. If cost is a genuine barrier, community mental health centers, graduate training clinics, and platforms like Open Path Collective (sessions starting around $30 to $80) are legitimate lower-cost options.

If you want to start building some CBT skills on your own while you're figuring out next steps, a workbook like *Mind Over Mood* by Christine Padesky is one of the best self-help resources grounded in actual clinical evidence. ## What medication actually does (and doesn't do)

SSRIs (the class that includes fluoxetine/Prozac, sertraline/Zoloft, escitalopram/Lexapro) are the most commonly prescribed antidepressants, and for good reason: they're reasonably effective, generally well-tolerated, and have decades of safety data. They don't work instantly. Most people need four to six weeks to assess whether a medication is doing anything meaningful, which is its own particular agony when you're suffering now.

Side effects are real and vary a lot by person and medication. Sexual side effects are common across SSRIs and are significantly underreported in clinical discussions (patients often don't bring it up unless directly asked). Weight changes, sleep disruption, and an initial increase in anxiety in the first week or two are also possible. None of this means you should avoid medication. It means you should talk to your prescribing doctor honestly about what you're experiencing instead of just quietly stopping, which is one of the more dangerous things people do.

One thing I'd push back on: the idea that starting antidepressants means you're on them forever. That's a fear I hear constantly. Most people with a first depressive episode who respond well to medication take it for six to twelve months and then taper off under medical supervision. People with recurrent depression may be recommended longer-term treatment, but that's a conversation to have with your doctor based on your specific history.

Psychiatrists prescribe medication. Your primary care doctor can too, and many people are first prescribed an antidepressant by their GP. But if you've tried one or two antidepressants without success, or if your situation is complex (bipolar disorder, ADHD alongside depression, significant trauma history), seeing an actual psychiatrist is worth the extra effort to find one.

## When combination treatment makes the most sense

I mentioned earlier that combination treatment is common among people who get better. Here's when the evidence most strongly supports it:

Severe or recurrent depression. Moderate depression that hasn't responded to one approach alone. Situations where someone needs faster symptom relief (medication can help stabilize someone faster) while also building coping skills and addressing underlying patterns (therapy's lane). Dysthymia, which is a lower-grade but persistent depression lasting two or more years, tends to respond particularly well to combined treatment.

There's a practical sequencing question too. Some people start medication, stabilize, and then begin therapy from a place where they can actually engage with it. Others start therapy and find that medication becomes necessary partway through when depression worsens. There's no single right sequence. What matters is that both options stay on the table.

The [National Alliance on Mental Illness (NAMI)](https://www.nami.org/) has a helpline (1-800-950-NAMI) and genuinely useful resources if you're trying to understand your options and don't know where to start. They're not just for people in crisis.

## The factors that actually help you decide

Your own preferences matter more than any guideline. If you are fundamentally opposed to medication, that opposition will likely undermine treatment even if you technically take it. Same with therapy: if you don't believe talking will help, that skepticism is a real variable.

Severity and urgency are probably the single most important factors. If you're having suicidal thoughts, please reach out to the [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/) by calling or texting 988. That's not a place to wait and see how therapy goes over the next few weeks.

Access and logistics matter, too. Therapy requires a consistent weekly commitment and a provider who actually has openings, which in many areas right now is harder than it sounds. Medication requires a prescriber, follow-up appointments, and willingness to stay with the process through an adjustment period. Neither option is frictionless.

Previous history is useful information. If you had a relative for whom a particular medication worked well, that's actually worth mentioning to a psychiatrist (there's some genetic component to medication response). If you tried CBT years ago and found it completely unhelpful, a different modality might serve you better now.
