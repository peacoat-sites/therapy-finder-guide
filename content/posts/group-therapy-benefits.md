Imagine sitting in a circle with seven strangers, all of you quiet at first, nobody sure who should speak. Then one person says something you've been too embarrassed to admit to anyone, even your closest friend. And you feel your shoulders drop about two inches. That's group therapy. It's unglamorous, sometimes awkward, and one of the most underrated tools in mental health care.

A lot of people dismiss group therapy before they've tried it. They picture a church basement, folding chairs, and a Styrofoam cup of bad coffee. Some groups do meet in exactly that setting, and they still change lives. But the dismissal usually comes from a misunderstanding of what group therapy actually does and how it's different from individual counseling. The research is compelling: a landmark meta-analysis published in the *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* found group therapy to be as effective as individual therapy for a wide range of conditions, including depression and anxiety. That's not a consolation prize. That's a meaningful clinical result.

## What Actually Happens in a Therapy Group

Group therapy isn't a support group, though both have value. A support group is typically peer-led, open-ended, and focused on shared experience. Group therapy is led by a licensed mental health professional, structured around specific therapeutic goals, and usually time-limited. Sessions typically run 60 to 90 minutes, with groups ranging from five to fifteen members.

There are several formats. Process groups focus on interpersonal dynamics, using what happens between group members as therapeutic material. Psychoeducational groups teach specific skills, like coping strategies for anxiety or communication techniques for managing conflict. Skills-based groups, such as those rooted in Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), follow a curriculum and are often closed, meaning the same members attend throughout the program. If you want to understand how skills-based groups work, our guide on [what DBT therapy is](/dbt-therapy-what-is-it/) breaks it down in plain language.

The therapist's role varies by format. In a process group, the therapist often holds back, letting the group interaction generate the therapeutic material. In a psychoeducational or skills group, the therapist is more active, closer to a teacher or coach. Both approaches work. The right fit depends on what you're trying to address.

Here's something individual therapy can't give you: the experience of being truly seen by multiple people at once, none of them paid to care about you.

Psychologist Irvin Yalom identified what he called the "therapeutic factors" that make group therapy work, and several of them are completely unique to the group setting. One is universality, the realization that you're not uniquely broken. Sitting across from someone who has the same intrusive thoughts, the same relationship patterns, or the same crushing self-doubt is genuinely therapeutic in a way that a therapist saying "this is very common" can't fully replicate. You feel it rather than just hearing it.

Another factor is altruism. When you help someone else in the group, you benefit too. Many people in therapy arrive with a diminished sense of self-worth. Being useful to another person, offering perspective, reflecting something back to them that they couldn't see, rebuilds something. It's hard to quantify but easy to recognize once you've watched it happen.

Then there's what Yalom called "corrective recapitulation of the primary family group." That's a dense phrase for a simple idea: many of our deepest wounds come from our original family system, and a therapy group recreates a similar interpersonal structure. Working through conflict, expressing a need, tolerating someone's disapproval without falling apart, those things can be practiced and processed in real time within the group.

I've seen clients make more progress on interpersonal issues in twelve weeks of group work than in a year of individual therapy. That's not a knock on individual therapy. It's a recognition that some problems are fundamentally relational, and you need other people present to address them.

## Who Benefits Most from Group Therapy

Group therapy works across a wide range of concerns. Depression, generalized anxiety, social anxiety, grief, trauma, eating disorders, substance use, anger, relationship difficulties. The National Alliance on Mental Illness (NAMI) recognizes group therapy as an effective evidence-based option for many of these conditions, and their resources at [nami.org](https://www.nami.org/) are a good starting point if you're trying to understand whether a particular concern is appropriate for group treatment.

Social anxiety is a particularly interesting case. For someone with social anxiety, being in a room with strangers sounds like the last thing they'd want to do. But that's exactly why it works. Exposure to the feared situation, combined with skilled therapeutic support and the lived evidence that other people accept you, is more effective than talking about social anxiety in a private office. It's active, not just reflective.

Group therapy is also well-suited for grief. Our [grief counseling guide](/grief-counseling-guide/) goes into depth on individual counseling options, but grief groups specifically can provide something individual therapy can't: the presence of others who have lost, who understand the strange, non-linear timeline of mourning without needing it explained.

It's also worth saying who might not be an ideal fit initially. Someone in acute psychiatric crisis, someone actively psychotic, or someone whose trauma is so raw that hearing others' stories would be retraumatizing, these individuals may need individual stabilization first. A good therapist or psychiatrist can help you figure out the right sequence.

## How Group Therapy Compares to Individual Therapy

This isn't really a competition. Many people do both simultaneously, seeing an individual therapist and attending a group. But if you're weighing options, here's a practical comparison.

| Factor | Individual Therapy | Group Therapy |
|---|---|---|
| Cost per session | Typically higher | Typically lower per person |
| Focus | Tailored entirely to you | Shared with others |
| Interpersonal practice | Limited | High: real-time with peers |
| Scheduling flexibility | Often more flexible | Fixed time, fixed group |
| Waitlist availability | Varies widely | Often shorter waits |
| Insurance coverage | Widely covered | Usually covered (verify first) |
| Best for | Deep individual work, trauma processing | Interpersonal issues, isolation, skill-building |

Cost is real. Group therapy is generally more affordable than individual therapy because the therapist's time is divided among several people. ## How to Find and Start a Therapy Group

The process is more straightforward than most people expect.

**Step 1: Identify what you're looking for.** Are you dealing with a specific issue like grief, anxiety, or relationship patterns? Or are you looking for general process group work? Knowing this narrows your search considerably.

**Step 2: Talk to your current provider.** If you already have an individual therapist or psychiatrist, ask them directly. Many clinicians run groups themselves or have referral relationships with group facilitators. This is the fastest route.

**Step 3: Search targeted directories.** Psychology Today's therapist finder lets you filter by group therapy specifically. The American Group Psychotherapy Association (AGPA) has a group locator at agpa.org. NAMI local chapters often know of community-based groups in your area.

**Step 4: Call and ask the right questions.** When you contact a group facilitator, ask: Is this a closed or open group? How many members are in the group currently? What theoretical approach does the group use? What's the commitment, and what happens if I need to leave early? A good facilitator will welcome these questions.

**Step 5: Expect an intake or screening.** Most structured therapy groups screen new members individually before they join. This protects the existing group and helps ensure you're a good fit. It's not a rejection, it's a clinical process.

**Step 6: Commit to at least four to six sessions before deciding.** The first session of group therapy is almost always uncomfortable. You don't know the norms, the people, or the rhythm yet. Discomfort early is not a sign it isn't working. Give it real time.

If you're looking for tools to support your work between sessions, structured journals and workbooks can be genuinely useful. Titles like *The CBT Workbook for Mental Health* by Simon Rego and Sarah Fader are available on Amazon and can help you process group sessions between meetings.
