Maybe you've been in therapy before. Maybe it helped, maybe it didn't, or maybe life just got busy and you stopped going. Now it's months or years later and you're wondering if you should try again. That particular kind of wondering, the quiet "should I go back?" that surfaces at 11pm on a Tuesday, is something I've heard described more times than I can count. And the fact that you're asking at all usually means the answer is yes.

But let's actually talk about what returning to therapy looks like, because it's genuinely different from starting for the first time. Most advice treats them the same way. They're not.

## The guilt and the gap are normal

Your therapist is not disappointed in you. That fear, the one that sounds like "I let things get bad again" or "I should have kept this up", is one of the most common things I hear from people considering a return. It's almost always unfounded.

Therapy isn't a gym membership where you're shamed for missing sessions. Life interrupts. Insurance runs out. Kids happen. Moves happen. Sometimes you felt better and decided you were done, which was reasonable at the time. None of that means you failed.

And if you stopped because the therapist wasn't a good fit? That's actually useful information. You already know what didn't work. That's more than most first-timers have.

## Going back to the same therapist vs. finding someone new

This is the first real decision, and I have a clear opinion: if your previous therapist was genuinely helpful, reach out to them first. Don't overthink it. A simple "I've been away for a while and I'd like to come back" message through their portal or a quick call is all it takes. Most therapists have people return after gaps of a year, two years, even longer. It's not unusual. You won't have to start completely from scratch, which matters more than people realize, especially if you put real work into building that therapeutic relationship.

If your previous therapist wasn't a good fit, or has retired, or moved practices, you're in a search. Psychology Today's therapist directory is genuinely one of the better places to start. You can filter by insurance, specialty, location, and therapy type all at once, which saves you a lot of phone tag.

One thing worth considering: if you've been away for several years, your needs might have shifted. Someone who needed support through a divorce might need something very different now. Being honest with yourself about what's actually going on right now will help you find the right type of therapy faster.

## What type of therapy, and does it matter?

It does. Though not as dramatically as some people fear.

If you had CBT (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy) before and found it too structured or too homework-heavy, that's worth knowing. CBT is skills-based and often shorter-term, usually 12 to 20 sessions. It works very well for anxiety and depression, and there's strong research behind it. But it asks things of you between sessions. If that felt like too much pressure last time, a more open-ended talk therapy approach might suit you better now.

EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing) has gotten more mainstream attention in the last few years. For people returning to therapy specifically to address trauma they didn't fully process before, it's worth asking a prospective therapist about. The research support has grown considerably since the early 2000s, and a 2020 meta-analysis in the Journal of Anxiety Disorders confirmed its effectiveness for PTSD across multiple population groups.

For people who want something in between sessions, especially while you're on a waitlist or getting started, there are solid CBT-based workbooks that can help. The Cognitive Behavioral Workbook for Depression by William Knaus (around $18-20 on Amazon) is one I've recommended many times over the years. It's not a replacement for therapy, but it can help you start identifying patterns before you even sit down with a therapist.

## The practical stuff nobody talks about

Insurance is confusing. The mental health parity laws that are supposed to make mental health coverage equivalent to medical coverage don't always play out that way in practice. Before you book anything, call your insurance company and ask: does my plan cover outpatient mental health, what's my copay, and do I have a deductible I need to meet first?

If cost is a barrier, community mental health centers often offer sliding-scale fees. The National Alliance on Mental Illness (NAMI) has a helpline at 1-800-950-6264 and can help connect you with lower-cost options in your area. Steer there before assuming therapy is out of reach.

Telehealth is also genuinely good now. It's not just a pandemic workaround anymore. For people returning to therapy after a long break, the lower logistical burden of not commuting to an office can be the difference between actually going and not going.

## When you sit down for that first session back

Be honest about the gap. You don't owe anyone a detailed explanation, but telling your therapist (new or returning) "I've been away from therapy for three years and here's roughly why" gives them something to work with. If you're with someone new, they'll probably do an intake anyway. Don't be tempted to downplay where you are right now.

A lot of people present a slightly more "together" version of themselves in the first session back. It's understandable. It also slows things down. The room is a safe place to be specific about what's actually happening.

You've probably been thinking about this longer than you realize. The return doesn't have to be dramatic or perfectly timed. Sometimes it's just making one phone call on a Wednesday afternoon.
