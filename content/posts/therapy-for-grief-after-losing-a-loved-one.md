Grief doesn't follow a schedule. And if you're reading this, you're probably somewhere in the thick of it — maybe weeks out from a loss, maybe years, wondering why it still hits you sideways when you open a cabinet and see their coffee mug. You might be wondering whether what you're feeling is "normal," or whether it's finally time to talk to someone. Both questions are worth sitting with.

Here's what I tell people who come to me at this stage: there's no wrong time to seek support. But there is a real difference between the grief that moves through you, even painfully, and the grief that starts to calcify — the kind that quietly takes over your sleep, your relationships, your ability to picture any future at all. Therapy can help with both, though it looks different depending on where you are.

## What Grief Therapy Actually Is (and Isn't)

A lot of people come in expecting a therapist to help them "get over it." That framing is worth throwing out immediately. The goal of grief therapy isn't to stop loving someone or to stop missing them. It's to find a way to carry the loss without being crushed by it.

Grief therapy is a specific application of talk therapy, usually drawing on several different treatment approaches depending on what's happening for a particular person. It's not the same as crisis counseling. It's not just venting into a professionally licensed void. A skilled grief therapist will help you understand what's happening emotionally, identify where you're stuck, work through things like guilt or anger or relief (yes, relief — that one comes up more than people admit), and eventually help you rebuild some sense of what life looks like now.

One thing I want to be honest about: not every therapist who lists "grief" on their profile has deep training in it. When you're looking for someone, it's worth asking specifically whether they work with bereavement regularly and what approaches they use. You deserve a real answer to that question, not a vague reassurance.

This is where things get a little technical, but stay with me, because knowing the difference genuinely helps you find the right fit.

**Grief-Focused CBT (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy)** works on the thoughts and behaviors that have grown up around the loss. If you've started avoiding places, stopped doing things you used to love, or find yourself caught in a loop of "I should have known" or "I could have done more" — that's exactly the terrain CBT covers. It's structured. There's homework. Some people love that; others find it too clinical right after a major loss. A 2021 meta-analysis in *Clinical Psychology Review* found CBT-based approaches had significant effects on complicated grief symptoms specifically, which is worth knowing.

**Complicated Grief Treatment (CGT)**, developed by Dr. M. Katherine Shear at Columbia University, is a specific 16-session protocol designed for what clinicians now often call "prolonged grief disorder." This is grief that hasn't moved, even after a year or more, and that's significantly impairing daily functioning. If you feel genuinely stuck — not just sad, but unable to accept the reality of the death at some level, or feeling like life has no point without this person — CGT might be the most targeted option available. It's not widely offered everywhere, but the Columbia Center for Complicated Grief maintains a directory of trained clinicians.

**Narrative therapy** is something I find genuinely underused in grief work. The basic idea is that you're the author of the story of your life, and a loss can feel like the story has been violently rewritten without your consent. Narrative therapy helps you re-engage with authorship — not by pretending the loss didn't happen, but by finding where the person you lost still lives in who you are.

**EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing)** sounds strange if you haven't encountered it before, and I remember being skeptical the first time a colleague described it to me. But when grief is entangled with trauma — a sudden or violent death, a prolonged illness you witnessed, an accident — EMDR has a reasonably strong evidence base for processing the traumatic elements specifically. It's not a fit for every grief situation, but for grief with that traumatic edge, it can do things talk therapy alone sometimes can't reach.

Group therapy and support groups deserve a mention here too, though they're not the same thing. A grief support group (often peer-led, like those run through hospices or community centers) offers connection and normalization. Grief group therapy, led by a licensed therapist, adds clinical structure. For some people, hearing someone else say out loud the exact thing they were ashamed to think is worth more than months of one-on-one sessions. These two approaches work well in combination.

## Prolonged Grief: When to Take It More Seriously

Grief is supposed to hurt. I want to be clear about that, because there's an unfortunate cultural tendency to pathologize sadness too quickly and, paradoxically, to tell people to "move on" before they're ready.

But prolonged grief disorder — formally recognized in the DSM-5-TR in 2022 — is real, and it affects roughly 10% of bereaved people. The markers aren't just "still sad after a year." They include intense longing that doesn't ease, difficulty accepting the death, feeling like part of yourself died, inability to trust others since the loss, bitterness or anger that hasn't softened, and significant interference with daily functioning. If those phrases describe what's been happening for you, please don't wait longer to find a therapist. The [National Alliance on Mental Illness (NAMI)](https://www.nami.org/) has a helpline (1-800-950-NAMI) and a wealth of resources specifically for navigating prolonged and complex grief.

There's also an important intersection with suicidal thinking. Grief, especially after a traumatic or unexpected loss, can sometimes go to very dark places. If you're having thoughts of not wanting to be here, the [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/) is available 24/7 by call or text. Grief that touches that territory needs professional support, not just time.

## The Practical Questions: Finding Someone and Paying for It

Okay, so you've decided you want to try therapy. Here's where a lot of people stall out.

Finding a grief therapist specifically: Psychology Today's therapist directory lets you filter by specialty, including "grief." The Association for Death Education and Counseling (ADEC) maintains a directory of certified grief counselors at adec.org. Your primary care doctor can often give referrals too, and hospice organizations in your area may offer free or low-cost bereavement counseling to anyone in the community, not just families of their patients. That last one is widely underused.

Cost is a real concern and I won't pretend otherwise. Individual therapy in most U.S. cities runs roughly $120-$250 per session without insurance. Many therapists offer sliding scale fees; it's completely reasonable to ask. Community mental health centers charge on income-based scales. If you have insurance, mental health benefits have improved significantly since the Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act, though applying them still requires navigating insurance bureaucracy that can be genuinely exhausting when you're already depleted. Open Path Collective (openpathcollective.org) is a network where therapists offer sessions at $30-$80 for people who qualify.

Between sessions, some people find structured workbooks genuinely useful as a complement to therapy (not a replacement). [The Grief Recovery Handbook by John W. James and Russell Friedman](https://www.amazon.com/Grief-Recovery-Handbook-20th-Anniversary/dp/0061686077?tag=thementalhealt-20) has been around for decades for good reason. ## What to Expect in the First Few Sessions

People are often surprised that the first session isn't as hard as they feared, and then surprised again when session three or four hits them harder than they expected. That's normal.

Early sessions are largely about history: the relationship, the circumstances of the death, what the loss has felt like, what's been working and what hasn't. A good therapist won't push you to process more than you're ready to process in the first meeting. They're also assessing what kind of grief this is and what approaches might fit.

You might feel emotionally exhausted after sessions, especially early ones. Build in some buffer time afterward if you can. Don't schedule a major work meeting directly after your first few appointments.

You also have every right to change therapists if something isn't clicking by session four or five. The relationship between you and a therapist is a real factor in outcomes — research on this is consistent. A therapist who specializes in grief but doesn't feel like a good fit for you specifically isn't the right therapist.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does grief therapy usually last?

There's no universal timeline, but many people see meaningful movement in 12-20 sessions of focused grief therapy. Complicated grief treatment is a structured 16-session protocol. If your grief is less entrenched, you might find shorter-term work helpful; some people return for additional support at difficult anniversaries or milestones.

### Is grief therapy different from regular talk therapy?

Yes, in practice, though not always structurally. Grief-specialized therapists have training in bereavement-specific models, trauma-informed approaches, and the clinical difference between normal grief and prolonged grief disorder. A general therapist can offer meaningful support, but if your grief is complex or prolonged, someone with specific bereavement training will likely serve you better.

### Do I have to talk about the person who died in every session?

No, and a good therapist won't force the narrative. Some sessions will focus on practical things like sleep, routine, relationship strain, or how to handle a specific upcoming event. The loss will be present in the background always, but grief therapy isn't just a loop of recounting what happened.

### Can grief therapy help even if it's been years since the loss?

Yes. Grief doesn't expire, and neither does the value of processing it. Some people come to therapy five or ten years after a loss when something — a new loss, a life transition, a milestone the person didn't live to see — reopens it. That's not weakness or failure; that's how grief works for some people.

### What if I'm not ready to talk to a therapist yet?

That's a real place to be, and it's okay to sit there for a while. Reading, journaling, peer support groups, and connecting with community can all help in the meantime. Just watch for signs that the grief is getting heavier rather than lighter over time. If it is, that's information worth acting on.
